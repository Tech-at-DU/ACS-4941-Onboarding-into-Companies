## Why you should know this

Take-homes and timed problems are two of the most common technical interview formats, and both improve with practice and feedback. Today you'll get detailed feedback on your take-home and comparison with your AI-assisted one, plus two more problems solved in class. You'll also get a short preview of first-round screens, including ones run by AI, which you'll practice on your own this week.

## Learning Objectives

1. Present and defend a take-home submission the way you would in a follow-up interview
1. Compare your work with and without AI, and decide what to practice without it
1. Describe the formats of first-round screens, including AI phone screens and recorded video interviews
1. Apply a repeatable sequence to solve a timed problem: clarify, examples, brute force, optimize, code, test
1. Recognize the pattern underneath a problem that's described as a product story

1. Takes follow-up questions from classmates and the instructor, as in a real take-home review (5 min). For example: "Why did you structure it this way?" "What happens with bad input?" "What would you test next?" "Add this small feature — how would you do it?"

**AI vs. no-AI comparison (9 min).** As a group:

- Where did you depend on AI more than you realized? Where was it genuinely faster?
- What should you practice *without* AI before interviews? (Most interview loops still include at least one no-AI round.)

## Overview: First-Round Screens (10 min)

Read together: [AI in the Job Search, Section 4](../Resources/AI_In_The_Job_Search.md#4-ai-run-interviews-ai-screens).

- **What a first screen checks:** Are you a real, qualified candidate? Can you communicate clearly? Are salary, location, work authorization, and timing compatible?
- **Formats:**
  - **Recruiter call:** a person talks with you for 15–30 minutes, mostly about fit and logistics
  - **AI phone screen:** an AI voice asks similar questions, sometimes basic technical ones
  - **AI chat screen:** the same questions by text
  - **Async video interview:** you record answers to preset questions, often with 30 seconds to prepare, 1–2 minutes to answer, and limited retakes
- **How to do well:** keep answers to 60–90 seconds, lead with the point, give one example, and use language from the job posting where it honestly fits. On video, look at the camera lens, not your own face on screen.
- **Your rights:** some places (Illinois, New York City) require employers to tell you when AI is used in hiring decisions. It's fine to ask a recruiter, "Is this screen reviewed by a person?"
- **Salary question:** find the posted pay range for the role before any screen. California, Colorado, New York, and Washington require postings to include one.

You'll run three practice screens on your own this week. See [AI Screen Practice](../Assignments/AI_Screen_Practice.md).

## Overview: A Problem-Solving Sequence (5 min)

A quick preview of the sequence we'll go deeper on in Session 7:

1. **Clarify:** restate the problem; ask about input size and edge cases
1. **Examples:** work 1–2 by hand, including an edge case
1. **Brute force:** describe the simplest approach and its Big O (how time and memory grow with input size)
1. **Optimize:** look for a better approach. What pattern is this, underneath the story?
1. **Code:** narrate as you go
1. **Test:** walk through your code with an example; check edge cases

## We Do: Solve a Problem Together (25 min)

Problem: **W-4: Concert Ticket Resale** from the [Problem Bank](../Resources/Problem_Bank.md#w-4-concert-ticket-resale).

- One student drives (types) while the others direct. Rotate the driver every 7–8 minutes.
- The instructor plays interviewer: answers clarifying questions, and pauses the group at each step of the sequence to ask "what's next?"
- Before coding, the group must agree on the brute-force approach and its complexity, then find the better one.
- No AI.

**Instructor note:** if a student already solved W-4 for the Week 1 diagnostic, make them the interviewer for this problem and pick another warm-up for the group.

## You Do: Timed Solo Problem (35 min)

Each student solves a **different** problem from the interview-level section of the [Problem Bank](../Resources/Problem_Bank.md#interview-level-use-for-week-4). Suggested: **I-2 Trending Tags**, **I-4 Calendar Busy Blocks**, or **I-9 Parking Garage Ramps**.

- 30 minutes, no AI, plain editor.
- Write your steps from the sequence as comments before you code: clarifying assumptions, examples, brute force, then your chosen approach.
- Last 5 minutes: write the time and space complexity and one thing you'd do differently.
- The instructor circulates. If you're stuck for more than 5 minutes, say what you've tried and ask for a hint, the same way you would in an interview.

## Share Solutions & Debrief (20 min)

Each student (about 6 minutes):

1. Reads their problem's story aloud, then names the pattern underneath it and what gave it away.
1. Walks through the solution and its complexity.
1. Classmates ask one interviewer-style question each.

Then, as a group: Which step of the sequence did you skip under time pressure? (Usually clarifying and testing.)

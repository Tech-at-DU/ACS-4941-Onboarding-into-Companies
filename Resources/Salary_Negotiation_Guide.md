# Salary Negotiation & Offer Evaluation Guide

Most entry-level candidates don't negotiate, and most employers expect at least a polite ask. A respectful negotiation almost never costs you an offer. This guide covers how to evaluate an offer, how to negotiate, and the things beyond salary that matter.

---

## 1. Know your numbers before the first call

Research a realistic range for the role, level, and location **before** any recruiter asks about salary.

**Sources:**

- **Levels.fyi** — self-reported compensation by company and level
- **Glassdoor** and **Indeed** salary pages
- **Pay transparency laws** — California, Colorado, New York, Washington, and several other states require salary ranges in job postings. Use them.
- **Alumni and your network** — ask "what's a typical range for a junior engineer at companies like yours?" (not "what do you make?")
- **AI tools** — useful for summarizing what you found, but don't trust salary numbers an AI gives you without checking a real source. They are often outdated or invented.

**Write down three numbers:**

| Number | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Walk-away** | The lowest offer you'd accept, based on your real expenses and alternatives |
| **Target** | A fair number based on your research |
| **Anchor** | A justifiable number at the top of the range — what you'll ask for |

---

## 2. Handling the salary question early

Recruiters (and AI screens) often ask for salary expectations in the first call.

- **If a range is posted:** "Based on the posted range and my research, I'm targeting the upper half of that range, but I'm flexible depending on the full package."
- **If no range is posted:** "I'd like to learn more about the role first. Could you share the budgeted range for this position?"
- **If they insist on a number:** give a researched range, with the bottom of your range at or above your target.

Avoid giving a single low number just to seem easy to hire.

## 3. Evaluating an offer: the full package

| Component | Questions to ask |
| --- | --- |
| **Base salary** | Is it in line with your research? |
| **Signing bonus** | Is there one? Does it have to be paid back if you leave early? |
| **Equity** | What type (stock options vs. RSUs — restricted stock units)? How many shares, what's the vesting schedule (usually 4 years with a 1-year "cliff"), and what's the company's latest valuation? |
| **Bonus** | Is there an annual bonus? Is it guaranteed or based on performance? |
| **Benefits** | Health, dental, vision costs; retirement match (401k); paid time off |
| **Learning budget** | Conferences, courses, books, AI tool subscriptions |
| **Location & RTO** | RTO = return to office. How many days on-site? Is that likely to change? |
| **Title & level** | Junior / Engineer I / Associate — affects your next salary |
| **Start date** | Flexible? |
| **Stability** | Recent layoffs? Funding runway (how many months of money a startup has left)? |
| **Visa sponsorship** | If you need it — will they sponsor, and in writing? (See Section 6.) |

### Equity basics, in plain terms

- **Stock options:** the right to *buy* shares later at a fixed price. Only valuable if the company's value grows and you can eventually sell (usually via acquisition or IPO). Treat startup options as a lottery ticket, not salary.
- **RSUs:** shares given to you as they vest. Common at public companies. Worth real money as soon as they vest.
- **Vesting:** you earn equity over time. A "1-year cliff" means you get nothing if you leave before 12 months.

**Questions to ask about startup equity:** How many total shares are outstanding? What's the strike price? What was the last valuation? How long do I have to exercise options if I leave?

## 4. Contract and contract-to-hire roles

Contract roles are more common for entry-level developers in this market. They can be a good door in — evaluate them carefully.

| Question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| W-2 or 1099? | W-2 contractors are employees of a staffing agency (taxes withheld, sometimes benefits). 1099 means you're self-employed — you pay both halves of payroll tax (roughly an extra 7.65%) and get no benefits. |
| Hourly rate? | Convert to annual: hourly × 2,080 hours. Then subtract the value of benefits you're not getting. A rule of thumb is that a contract rate should be 25–40% higher than the equivalent salary. |
| Length? | 3, 6, 12 months? Extensions likely? |
| Conversion terms? | "Contract-to-hire" — how many people actually convert? At what salary? After how long? Ask for specifics. |
| Who are you working for? | The agency, the client, or both? Who do you go to with problems? |

## 5. How to negotiate

### The basic script

1. **Thank them and show enthusiasm.** "Thank you — I'm really excited about this role and the team."
2. **Ask for time.** "Could I have until Friday to review the full offer?" (2–5 business days is normal.)
3. **Make your ask, with a reason.** "Based on my research for this role in this area, and the deployed projects and experience I'm bringing, I was hoping for a base salary closer to $X. Is there flexibility there?"
4. **Stop talking.** Let them respond.
5. **If base salary is fixed,** ask about other parts: "I understand. Is there flexibility on the signing bonus, start date, or learning budget?"
6. **Get the final offer in writing** before you accept.

### Do

- Negotiate by phone or video when possible, then confirm in writing
- Be polite and collaborative — you'll work with these people
- Use real competing offers if you have them (never invent one)
- Negotiate once, clearly — don't drag it out over many rounds

### Don't

- Accept on the spot
- Lie about other offers or your current salary (in many states, employers can't ask your salary history anyway)
- Make ultimatums you won't follow through on
- Apologize for asking

### Exploding offers

An "exploding offer" has a very short deadline (24–48 hours). It's fine to ask for more time: "I want to give this the consideration it deserves. Could I have until [date]?"

## 6. Work authorization for international students

If you're on an F-1 student visa, work authorization affects which jobs you can take and when. **Rules changed significantly in 2025 and may change again. Confirm current rules with your school's international student office before making decisions.**

- **OPT** (Optional Practical Training) lets F-1 students work in the U.S. in their field for 12 months after graduation. Apply early — processing takes time.
- **STEM OPT** extends OPT by 24 more months for STEM degrees, but the employer must use E-Verify and sign a training plan (Form I-983). Ask employers early whether they support STEM OPT.
- **H-1B** is the main long-term work visa. It's issued through an annual lottery, and in 2025 the U.S. government added a large new fee on certain new H-1B petitions and moved toward changing how the lottery selects applicants. These changes make many employers more cautious about sponsorship. Ask directly and early: "Does the company sponsor visas for this role?"
- **Cap-gap** protection may extend your work authorization between OPT ending and an H-1B starting. Ask your international student office.

## 7. Verbal assessment questions

Your [negotiation assessment](../Assignments/Negotiation.md) will include questions like these. Be ready to answer them out loud.

1. What three numbers should you know before a salary conversation, and how do you find them?
2. A recruiter asks for your salary expectations on the first call. What do you say?
3. What's the difference between stock options and RSUs?
4. What does a 4-year vesting schedule with a 1-year cliff mean?
5. You're offered $40/hour on a 1099 contract. Roughly what's that worth compared to a salaried role, and why?
6. What questions do you ask about a contract-to-hire role?
7. The recruiter says base salary is fixed. What else can you negotiate?
8. You get an offer with a 24-hour deadline. What do you do?
9. Name three things besides salary that affect whether an offer is a good one.
10. How should you use AI tools in salary research, and what's the risk?

# SPD 2.41 Onboarding Into Companies

## Course Description

This course is a structured, accountability-driven job search sprint for developers who are close to graduation. It's built for the 2026/2027 hiring market, which looks different from the market this course was originally written for:

- **AI has changed the applicant pool.** Everyone can generate a resume and grind LeetCode with an assistant. The bar for standing out has moved to demonstrable projects, real networking, and the ability to *use* AI tools well in an interview — not just produce polished-looking output.
- **Interview loops are more varied.** Many companies have moved away from pure whiteboard algorithms toward practical/take-home exercises, live debugging, AI-paired coding rounds, and lightweight system design — even for junior roles. Classic DSA rounds haven't disappeared (they're still common at larger companies), but they're one format among several now.
- **Cold applications convert poorly.** Referrals, warm outreach, and a visible body of work matter more than volume-applying through job boards. Students need to build a pipeline, not just submit forms.
- **Entry-level hiring is more competitive and slower.** Loops are longer, "ghost" postings are common, and resilience/pipeline management is a real skill, not a soft add-on.

The course runs a short kickoff workshop to get every student's portfolio, resume, and LinkedIn into shape, then moves through networking, technical interviewing (across formats), system design fundamentals, behavioral interviewing, and negotiation. ACS staff work closely with students throughout and provide warm connections into our network — alumni, startups, YC companies, and larger tech employers.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of the course, you will be able to ...

1. Build and maintain a job-search-ready portfolio, GitHub profile, resume, and LinkedIn presence targeted at your goals
1. Run a proactive job search: sourcing real (non-ghost) roles, building a referral pipeline, and conducting effective warm outreach and informational interviews
1. Practice technical interviews across the formats you'll actually see in 2026/2027: algorithmic/DSA, practical or take-home style coding, live debugging, and AI-collaborative coding rounds
1. Explain and defend a junior-level system design decision, and reason about basic tradeoffs (data modeling, API design, scaling at a small scale)
1. Use AI coding and job-search tools effectively and honestly — knowing when disclosure is expected, and being able to explain and extend AI-assisted work without it
1. Practice behavioral and informational interview scenarios, and communicate clearly with a variety of personalities and interview styles (including remote/async formats)
1. Evaluate a job offer and negotiate compensation, including base, equity, and contract/contract-to-hire terms

### Technical Interview Practice (Weekly Challenge Rotation)

Real interview loops mix formats and show up on no fixed schedule — this assignment mirrors that instead of grinding one problem type. Starting Week 1, you're assigned one interview-format challenge in the week's first session and debrief it in the second. The challenge runs as a parallel track alongside whatever else that week covers — it does not need to match that week's headline topic.

| Week | Challenge Type | Review |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Diagnostic: timed DSA problem (baseline, low stakes) | Rubric-scored |
| 2 | Take-home challenge — AI use allowed and must be disclosed | Rubric-scored, group debrief |
| 3 | Take-home challenge — no AI allowed | Rubric-scored, group debrief |
| 4 | Timed DSA under interview conditions + debug-an-unfamiliar-codebase | Rubric-scored, group debrief |
| 5 | Live AI-pairing round (interviewer + AI assistant, live) | 1:1 instructor debrief — higher stakes, feeds into readiness for Week 6's full mock loop |

Submit each week's challenge through the [tracker](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/177VINzmBa23oLl5ndTbIE3ic5Dhv9L4JBclCDdLQnk4/edit#gid=136456159) before its debrief session. Pull problems from a bank spanning all five formats above — [this problem list](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1oQt5Li1zVrY-x0Ti2CGnRE0O-nUp8Hp3jsUkDRzt4Ss/edit#heading=h.e0c7yc48cw75) is a starting source and needs to be expanded to cover the take-home and AI-pairing formats. Meet a passing rubric standard on >70% of the five weekly challenges to satisfy this requirement. The Week 6 full mock interview loop is graded separately (see *In-Class Full Interview* below) — it's the capstone that synthesizes everything this rotation practiced.

## Interview Topics

### Algorithmic / Computer Science

Topics:
- arrays, strings, hashmaps
- linked lists, trees, graphs (basics)
- sorting and searching
- complexity analysis (time/space)
- dynamic programming (light coverage — most junior loops go no deeper than intro DP)

### Practical / Applied Coding

Topics:
- take-home style feature implementation
- reading and debugging unfamiliar/legacy code
- code review exercises (giving and receiving feedback)
- AI-collaborative pairing rounds: working with an AI assistant live, explaining and defending assistant-generated code, disclosure norms

### System Design (Junior Level)

Topics:
- basic API design (REST/GraphQL surface for a small feature)
- basic data modeling / schema design
- simple scaling tradeoffs (caching, indexing, read vs. write load) at a small-team scale

### Technical Discussion

Topics:
- sequencing an interview
- time management in an interview
- thinking out loud / narrating your process
- deep-diving your own resume and projects convincingly

### Behavioral

Topics:
- STAR method
- conflict resolution and working with a variety of personalities
- remote/hybrid and async team communication

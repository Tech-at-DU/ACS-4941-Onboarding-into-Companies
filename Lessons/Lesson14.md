## Why you should know this

Getting hired is the start, not the finish. New engineers who ramp up quickly earn trust, get better projects, and are in a stronger position when budgets get cut. Most courses stop at the offer. This one ends with what it actually takes to succeed once you're in — and with a plan to keep your search going after the term ends.

## Learning Objectives

1. Describe what typically happens in a new engineer's first 90 days and how to ramp up effectively
1. Navigate an unfamiliar codebase efficiently, using AI tools responsibly
1. Explain common workplace norms: PR etiquette, asking questions, 1:1s, performance reviews, and AI tool policies
1. Present a deployed project and defend its decisions

## Overview: Your First 90 Days (30 min)

**Week 1:**

- Getting your development environment working is often the hardest part. Write down every step as you go — then improve the onboarding docs for the next person. That's an easy, visible first contribution.
- Ship a tiny first change (a typo fix, a small bug) to learn the PR process end to end.
- **Find out the AI tool policy:** Which AI tools are approved or banned? Can you paste company code into external AI tools (often no, or only approved ones)? Should AI-generated code be labeled? How is it reviewed? Never assume your personal habits are allowed.

**Learning the codebase:**

- Trace one real request end to end, from the UI to the database and back.
- Read recent merged PRs (pull requests — proposed changes submitted for review) to learn team conventions.
- Use AI tools to explain unfamiliar code *if your company allows it* — then verify by reading and running the code.

**PR etiquette:**

- Small PRs get reviewed faster and better than big ones.
- Write a description: what changed, why, how you tested it, and screenshots for UI changes.
- If AI generated a meaningful part of the change, follow the team's norm for disclosing that.
- Respond to review comments graciously. Review comments are about code, not about you.

**Asking questions:**

- A common rule: try for 30–60 minutes, then ask — and include what you've already tried.
- Ask in public channels when possible; others probably have the same question.
- Keep a running doc of answers so you never ask the same thing twice.

**Working with your manager:**

- **1:1s** (one-on-one meetings with your manager): come with an agenda — progress, blockers, feedback, growth.
- Ask early: "What does success look like for me at 30, 60, and 90 days?"
- Keep a **brag document**: a running list of what you shipped, fixed, and learned. You'll need it for performance reviews, raises, and your next resume.

**Being a strong junior engineer in the AI era:** treat AI output like code from a fast but unreliable teammate — useful, but always reviewed. The juniors who stand out are the ones who catch what the AI missed, write tests, and ask good questions.

## Activity: Codebase Scavenger Hunt (20 min)

The instructor provides a mid-sized open source repository. In 15 minutes, each student answers:

1. How do you run it locally? (Find it, don't just guess.)
1. Where does a request for [a specific feature] enter the code, and where does it touch data?
1. What are the team's conventions for tests and PR descriptions? (Look at recent merged PRs.)
1. Find one small improvement you could make as a first contribution.

You may use an AI tool to help navigate, but you must verify each answer in the actual code. Spend the last 5 minutes comparing answers: where did the AI help, and where did it send someone the wrong way?

## Demo Day (30 min)

Each student (10 minutes each) presents their [proof-of-work project](../Assignments/Portfolio_Proof_of_Work.md#demo-day-session-14):

- **2 minutes:** live demo from the public URL
- **3 minutes:** walk through one decision from your `DECISIONS.md` — what you chose, the alternatives, and why
- **5 minutes:** questions from classmates and the instructor, just like an interviewer would ask

Invite ACS staff, alumni, or partner companies if possible. A demo in front of a real hiring manager is valuable practice — and sometimes leads somewhere.

## Retrospective: What Worked in Your Search (20 min)

- Which actions led to replies, conversations, or interviews? Which didn't? Look at your tracker numbers.
- What was the most useful thing you did this term?
- Where did AI help your search? Where did it hurt or waste time?
- What would you tell next term's students?

## Your Next 30 Days (20 min)

The term ends, but most job searches don't. Write a one-page plan:

1. Weekly targets for outreach, informational interviews, applications, and practice — continue your tracker
1. Three people you'll follow up with
1. One skill gap from your mock loop feedback, and how you'll close it
1. An accountability partner from class: agree to a weekly check-in (a 15-minute call or a message thread)
1. A date to check in with ACS staff

# SPD 2.41 Onboarding Into Companies

## Course Description

This course is a structured, accountability-driven job search sprint for developers who are close to graduation. It's built for the 2026/2027 hiring market, which looks very different from the market this course was originally written for:

- **AI has changed both sides of hiring.** Every applicant can generate a polished resume and practice coding problems with an AI assistant, and many employers now use AI to screen resumes and even run first-round phone or video interviews. Standing out now means showing real, deployed work, building real relationships, and being able to *explain and verify* your work — including work you built with AI help.
- **Interview loops are more varied.** Many companies have moved away from pure whiteboard algorithm questions toward take-home projects, live debugging, AI-assisted coding rounds (where you are expected to use an AI tool and are graded on how well you direct and check it), and lightweight system design — even for junior roles. Classic algorithm rounds still exist, especially at larger companies, but they're one format among several.
- **Cold applications convert poorly.** Referrals, warm outreach, and a visible body of work matter more than applying to hundreds of job-board postings. You need to build a pipeline, not just submit forms.
- **Entry-level hiring is more competitive and slower.** There are fewer junior software engineering openings, interview loops are longer, and "ghost" postings (listings for jobs that aren't really being filled) are common. Managing your pipeline and staying resilient through rejection are real skills.

The course starts by getting every student's portfolio, deployed project, resume, and LinkedIn into shape, then moves through networking, AI-run screening interviews, technical interviewing (across formats), system design fundamentals, behavioral interviewing, negotiation, and finally what it actually takes to succeed in your first 90 days on the job. ACS staff work closely with students throughout and provide warm connections into our network — alumni, startups, YC (Y Combinator, a well-known startup accelerator) companies, and larger tech employers.

Everything you need for this course lives in this repository: lessons, assignments, checklists, templates, and guides.

## What This Course Prioritizes

These are the highest-value things this course covers, in priority order. When time is tight, we protect the items at the top of the list.

1. **A real pipeline, built in class.** Outreach messages sent, referrals requested, interviews booked. We track *inputs* (actions you control) every session, because consistent action is what actually gets people hired.
1. **Lots of mock interview reps with feedback.** Peer mock interviews start in Week 3 and run most weeks, not just one mock at the end.
1. **One strong proof-of-work project.** A deployed project with a live URL, a clear README, and a written explanation of your decisions — something you can confidently talk about for 20 minutes.
1. **AI skills for the job search and interviews.** How to use AI tools honestly and effectively to prepare, how to succeed in interviews that require AI tools, how to handle interviews conducted by AI, and where AI use crosses the line.
1. **Onboarding itself.** What your first 90 days at a company look like and how to ramp up fast — the part most courses skip.
1. **Offer evaluation and negotiation.** Salary, equity, contract-to-hire terms, and the less obvious things (layoff risk, return-to-office policy, visa sponsorship).

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of the course, you will be able to ...

1. Build and maintain a job-search-ready portfolio, GitHub profile, resume, and LinkedIn presence, anchored by at least one deployed project you can explain in depth
1. Run a proactive job search: sourcing real (non-ghost) roles across a range of entry-level job titles, building a referral pipeline, and conducting effective warm outreach and informational interviews
1. Perform in technical interviews across the formats you'll actually see in 2026/2027: algorithm and data structure problems, take-home projects, live debugging, code review, and AI-assisted coding rounds
1. Explain and defend a junior-level system design decision, including a simple feature that uses an LLM (large language model, the kind of AI behind tools like ChatGPT and Claude)
1. Use AI tools effectively and honestly throughout the job search — knowing what employers expect, when to disclose AI use, how to handle AI-run screening interviews, and how to explain and extend AI-assisted work without the tool
1. Communicate clearly in behavioral, recruiter, and informational interviews, including remote and asynchronous (recorded, not live) formats
1. Evaluate a job offer and negotiate compensation, including base salary, equity, and contract or contract-to-hire terms
1. Create a 30-60-90 day onboarding plan and describe how to ramp up effectively in a new engineering team

## AI Policy for This Course

AI is part of every stage of hiring now, so this course treats AI skills as a core subject, not an afterthought. The full guide is [AI in the Job Search](Resources/AI_In_The_Job_Search.md). The short version:

- **Encouraged:** using AI to practice interviews, critique your resume, research companies, draft (then rewrite) outreach, explain unfamiliar code, and quiz yourself.
- **Allowed with disclosure:** AI-assisted work on assignments marked "AI allowed." Add a short note saying which tool you used and what for. This is the same habit employers expect.
- **Not allowed:** AI on challenges marked "no AI," and any tool that secretly feeds you answers during a live interview — mock or real. Employers actively detect these tools and candidates who use them get rejected or blacklisted.
- **The test that matters:** you must be able to explain, modify, and defend anything you submit without the AI in front of you.
- **Privacy:** never paste personal identifiers (Social Security number, home address, passwords) or a company's confidential material into an AI tool. Check your tool's data settings.

## Interview Topics

### Algorithms & Data Structures

Often called "DSA" (data structures and algorithms) in interview prep. Topics:

- arrays, strings, hash maps
- linked lists, trees, graphs (basics)
- sorting and searching
- complexity analysis: how running time and memory grow as input grows ("Big O" notation)
- dynamic programming (light coverage — most junior loops go no deeper than introductory problems)

### Practical / Applied Coding

Topics:

- take-home style feature implementation
- reading and debugging unfamiliar or legacy (older, inherited) code
- code review: giving and receiving feedback, including reviewing AI-generated code for bugs and security problems
- AI-assisted coding rounds: directing an AI coding assistant or agent, checking and testing its output, explaining and defending the result, and following disclosure norms

### System Design (Junior Level)

Topics:

- basic API design (the set of endpoints a small feature exposes, usually REST — a common style of web API built on URLs and HTTP methods like GET and POST)
- basic data modeling and database schema design
- simple scaling tradeoffs (caching, indexing, read-heavy vs. write-heavy load) at a small-team scale
- designing a simple LLM-powered feature: cost, speed, wrong answers, and how you'd test it

### Technical Discussion

Topics:

- sequencing an interview
- time management in an interview
- thinking out loud and narrating your process
- deep-diving your own resume and projects convincingly

### Behavioral

Topics:

- the STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result — a structure for answering "tell me about a time when…" questions)
- conflict resolution and working with a variety of personalities
- remote, hybrid, and asynchronous team communication
- talking honestly about how you use AI in your work

## Glossary

Terms and abbreviations used in this course, in plain language.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **30-60-90 plan** | A written plan for what you'll learn and accomplish in your first 30, 60, and 90 days at a new job. |
| **ACS** | Applied Computer Science, the program this course belongs to. |
| **AI agent / coding agent** | An AI tool that can take multi-step actions on its own — reading files, running commands, editing code — rather than just answering a question. Examples: Claude Code, Cursor's agent mode, GitHub Copilot's agent mode. |
| **AI screen** | A first-round interview conducted by AI software (by phone, video, or chat) instead of a human recruiter. |
| **API** | Application Programming Interface — a defined way for one piece of software to talk to another, such as a web server's endpoints. |
| **ATS** | Applicant Tracking System — the software companies use to collect, store, filter, and search job applications (examples: Greenhouse, Lever, Workday). |
| **Async video interview** | A recorded interview: you answer pre-set questions on camera, and a person or AI reviews the recording later. |
| **Big O** | Notation for how an algorithm's time or memory use grows as the input grows, e.g. O(n) grows linearly. |
| **Contract-to-hire** | A job that starts as a temporary contract with the possibility of converting to a full-time employee. |
| **Deploy / deployed** | Putting your app on the internet at a public URL so anyone can use it. |
| **DSA** | Data Structures and Algorithms — the classic computer science interview topics. |
| **Equity** | Partial ownership of a company, often given as stock options or RSUs (restricted stock units). |
| **FDE** | Forward-Deployed Engineer — an engineer who works directly with customers to build and customize solutions using the company's product. Common at AI companies. |
| **Ghost posting** | A job listing for a role that isn't actively being filled. |
| **H-1B** | A U.S. work visa that lets employers sponsor foreign workers in specialty occupations. |
| **Hiring committee** | A group of interviewers who review a candidate's whole interview packet together and decide whether to hire. |
| **LLM** | Large Language Model — the kind of AI behind ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini, trained on large amounts of text to understand and generate language. |
| **OPT / STEM OPT** | Optional Practical Training — lets international students on F-1 visas work in the U.S. after graduating, for 12 months, plus a 24-month extension for STEM degrees. |
| **PR** | Pull Request — a proposed code change submitted for review before it's merged into the main codebase. |
| **QA** | Quality Assurance — the work of testing software to find bugs before users do. |
| **RAG** | Retrieval-Augmented Generation — a way to make an AI answer questions using your own documents: first search for the relevant documents, then give them to the LLM along with the question so its answer is based on them. |
| **Referral** | When someone who works at a company recommends you for a role, usually through an internal system. Referred candidates are far more likely to get interviews. |
| **REST** | A common style of web API built on URLs and standard HTTP methods (GET, POST, PUT, DELETE). |
| **RTO** | Return to Office — a company's policy on how many days per week employees must work on-site. |
| **SDET** | Software Development Engineer in Test — a developer who writes code to test software automatically. |
| **STAR** | Situation, Task, Action, Result — a structure for answering behavioral interview questions. |
| **SWE** | Software Engineer. |
| **Take-home** | A coding project you complete on your own time (usually a few hours to a few days) and submit for review. |
| **Warm outreach** | Contacting someone you have some connection to (shared school, mutual contact, shared community) rather than a total stranger. |
| **YC** | Y Combinator — a well-known startup accelerator; "YC companies" are startups that went through its program. |
